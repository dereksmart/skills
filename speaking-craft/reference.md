# Speaking craft — distilled from Patrick Winston's *How to Speak*

A short, scannable digest of Winston's MIT lecture on giving talks. This is the deep-dive backing for [`SKILL.md`](SKILL.md) — when a deck audit or rehearsal needs more than the operator carries inline, look here. Speakers can also read it straight through.

Principles below are grouped by where they fire in the talk-building process. Within each section, hooks are short; the rationale is why it matters.

## Before the slides exist

**What really matters is what you know.** Winston's formula: K + P + T. Knowledge × practice, with a small coefficient on inherent talent. If the content isn't there, no delivery polish fixes it. Do the thinking first.

**Passion is the inspiration mechanism.** Freshmen remember teachers who told them they could do it; senior faculty remember people who helped them see a problem in a new way; everyone remembers speakers who visibly cared about the material. "Isn't that cool?" is a legitimate move.

## Openers

**Start with an empowerment promise, not a joke.** Tell the audience what they'll know at the end they don't know now. Jokes at the start fall flat because the room is still settling — putting laptops away, adjusting to your voice, tuning in. Winston's template: *"By the end of this, you will know X, and something among those things will make a difference."*

**Recognize collaborators on the first slide, not the last.** The last slide is real estate; don't give it away to a list of names.

## Structural heuristics (always on)

**Cycle.** Say the key idea three times, from different angles. At any moment ~20% of the audience is fogged out; cycling raises the probability that everyone catches it.

**Build a fence around your idea.** Differentiate it from adjacent ideas. *"This might look like Jones's approach, but his is exponential and mine's linear."* Prevents confusion with things you're not claiming.

**Verbal punctuation.** Provide landmark places where someone who drifted can get back on the bus. Enumerate. Reference the outline. *"We've covered one and two; third thing is…"* The seam is the handhold.

**Ask a question.** Engage the audience directly. Seven seconds is the outer edge of dead air you can hold while waiting for an answer — feels like an eternity but is standard. Pick questions carefully: not so obvious people are embarrassed to say, not so hard nobody can answer.

## Tools: boards, props, slides

**Boards beat slides for *teaching*. Slides are for *exposing*.** Board speed matches the rate at which people absorb ideas. Slides are fine for conference/job talks where the job is to surface ideas, not teach them.

**Props are disproportionately memorable.** Years later people remember the bicycle wheel demonstration, the pendulum at the nose, the broken pointer. If a physical object can anchor the concept, use it. The mechanism Winston proposes is *empathetic mirroring* — mirror neurons let the audience feel the action as if they were doing it, and that doesn't happen with slides.

**Use your hands as part of the talk.** Hands in pockets or behind the back reads as hiding a weapon in some cultures — and looks evasive everywhere. The board is a target; having something to point at solves the "what do I do with my hands" problem. Even pointing at things that aren't literally relevant can work (Papert did this deliberately).

## Slide crimes to avoid

**Too many slides, too many words.** Always. Winston's rule of thumb: *if you don't know what's wrong with someone's slides, start with "you have too many, and they have too many words."*

**Don't read your slides.** The audience knows how to read; it's insulting. Worse, people have one language processor — they're either reading *or* listening, not both. Dense slides make people read and tune you out. A study Winston cites found participants remembered what they *read* far better than what the speaker *said* when slides were word-heavy.

**The speaker and the slide can't be physically far apart.** That creates a tennis-match feeling where the audience's attention oscillates between you and the screen. Slides should be condiments to what you're saying.

**Font minimum: 40–50pt.** Anything below 35pt is usually a symptom of trying to fit too many words on a slide.

**Strip the chrome.** Get rid of: background junk, ornamental logos on every slide, slide titles you're already speaking aloud, unnecessary bullets, any decoration that isn't load-bearing.

**No laser pointers.** Turning to point loses eye contact — you end up talking to your slide. Put an arrow directly on the slide and refer to it.

**The too-heavy test.** Print the deck and lay it out on a table. If it reads as a dense grey brick, there isn't enough air. Imagery and whitespace are part of the content, not the absence of it.

**Hapax legomenon.** One impossibly-complex "look-how-tangled-this-is" slide per talk, at most. Used deliberately, for effect, once. The Afghanistan-governance diagram is the archetype.

## Informing vs persuading

Different talk types, different shapes.

**Informing (teaching, lecturing):** Start with the empowerment promise. Show passion — the audience calibrates on yours. Structure content as stories that can be retold. Use the board.

**Persuading (job talks, shop talks):** You have about **five minutes** to establish two things: (1) a vision — a problem someone cares about + a new angle on it, and (2) evidence you've *done* something — list the steps taken or to be taken to realize the vision. If those aren't clear in five, you've lost.

**Oral exams:** Most failures come from not *situating* (placing the work in context — why this, why now, what's been tried) and not *practicing* with non-experts. Experts and advisors hallucinate material that isn't actually in your talk; friends outside your field won't.

## Getting remembered — Winston's Star

Five S's that, together, make work stick:

- **Symbol** — a visual hook associated with the work (Winston's arch, for his thesis).
- **Slogan** — a short phrase that handles the idea ("one-shot learning").
- **Surprise** — a counter-intuitive result that challenges assumption.
- **Salient idea** — *one* idea that sticks out. Too many good ideas dilute; pick one to lead with.
- **Story** — how you got there. Why it matters. What you did.

Named deliberately for recall: *symbol, slogan, surprise, salient, story.*

## Closing

**Final slide: contributions.** It stays up during Q&A while people file out. Make it something they'd want to write down. Specifically NOT:

- A list of collaborators (puts a floor on how much of the work was "yours" — and collaborators belong on slide one)
- "Questions?" — squanders 20 minutes of real estate on a word
- URL / link dump — nobody writes them down
- "Conclusions" — people don't care about your conclusions; they care about what you *did*

**Final words: not "thank you."** It's a weak move. Implies the audience only stayed out of politeness. If applause starts, you can mouth it — but don't end your delivery on it.

**Good ways to stop:**
- A joke at the end is fine (audience has adjusted to you; the risk of the opening joke is gone). Winston quotes Doug Lenat: *"I always finish with a joke — that way people think they've had fun the whole time."*
- A benediction (religious, political, or ceremonial — depends on context).
- A **salute to the audience** — name what was valuable about this specific room, this specific time. *"It's been great to see what you're doing here. I've been provoked by your questions. I look forward to coming back."* Works almost anywhere.

## Quick reference card

A tight checklist for reviewing a draft talk:

- [ ] Opener: empowerment promise, not a joke, not a biography
- [ ] Collaborators on slide one (not the last)
- [ ] Key idea cycled 2–3 times from different angles
- [ ] Fences drawn around the idea — differentiated from adjacent work
- [ ] Verbal punctuation / outline returns every ~10 min
- [ ] At least one question to the room
- [ ] Slides: minimal words, ≥40pt font, no chrome, no laser pointer
- [ ] Props or physical demonstrations where they'd land (they're remembered disproportionately)
- [ ] Five S's present: symbol, slogan, surprise, salient idea, story
- [ ] Final slide = contributions (not "Questions?", not "Thank you", not links)
- [ ] Last spoken words ≠ "thank you"
